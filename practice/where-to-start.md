# Where to start

Setup is done. **This is a guide, not a set of instructions** — put together to help
you get the most out of your first week: what to connect, what to build, and what to
leave until later.

**The goal for your first day: get two systems connected.** Pick the two that matter
most — usually whatever runs the operation day to day, and wherever your customers
and leads live. **One per session**, so each one gets finished properly.

**Then pick one of these three as your first build.** Whichever you recognise
fastest is the right one:

1. **Two systems that have never talked to each other.** Now they can — so ask the
   thing you've always wanted to know and never been able to answer. The sharpest
   example: your ads platform can only ever tell you what a *lead* cost, because it
   never finds out who bought. Put it next to your CRM and whatever runs the
   operation and you get what a *customer* cost, which is the only number that
   matters.
2. **The report you put together by hand every week or month.** Explain it once,
   watch Claude do it, check it against a period you know, then have it arrive on
   its own without you lifting a finger.
3. **The thing about your systems that's annoyed you for years.** You've probably
   stopped noticing it. Point Claude at it and find out whether it's fixable.

## One session, one job

**One session, one job.** Every connection gets its own session. A connection that goes
badly doesn't drag the others down with it, the conversation stays short enough to
think in, and you finish each one with something actually working.

```
done already    Setup                                        ✅
next session       "I've just finished setting up"
                 → it checks everything, then takes your
                   systems list in the same session            ~5 min
then            "help me connect my [X]"   one session, one system
then            "help me connect my [Y]"   one session, one system
...
whenever        "My Business Interview"    its own session, your own time
```

Nothing here has to happen today, or in any particular order after the systems
list. Pick the one that would help most this week.

## Then one session per connection

> "Help me connect my [name the system]."

That's the whole instruction. It'll check whether there's a ready-made connection,
tell you what's involved before it starts, and walk you through the sign-in.

Your two jobs are signing in to your own accounts and clicking approve. Nothing
else.

When it's done, leave it and start a new session for the next one. No need to close anything.

## Keep the sessions tidy, or it gets messy fast

One session per connection means you'll have a lot of sessions. Five minutes of
housekeeping now saves you hunting through forty of them in a month.

**Rename them.** Claude names a session from whatever you said first, which is often
nothing like what it ended up being. Click the three dots next to the session and
rename it to what it actually is — *Connect the booking system*, *Monday numbers*, *My Business
Interview*.

**Make groups down the left.** Same place, three dots, move to group. Create these
two to start with:

```
1   Connections       one session per system you've connected
2   [Your business]   the day-to-day work — "The Cafe", "Smith Plumbing"
```

Add more when you need them — a *Reports* group earns its place once you've built
a couple of things, not before.

If you run more than one business, a group each — though if they're the same kind
of business, several locations of the same thing, one group usually covers it. And
remember the *folder* is what keeps them properly separate. Groups are just
tidiness; folders are what Claude can actually see.

**Do it as you go.** Finish a connection, rename the session, drop it in Connections,
move on.

**Why it's worth bothering:** when a connection breaks in March, you want to open the session
where you connected it and see exactly what you did. That's only possible if you
can find it.

### One thing to change in a week or two

You'll start in **Auto**, where Claude asks about the decisions that matter. Early on
every box teaches you something. Once you notice you're clicking allow without
reading, switch to **Bypass permissions** — Settings → Claude Code turns it on. That
shift comes on its own as you get more comfortable and confident in the work Claude
is producing for you.

Your safety rules don't change with the mode. Nothing goes out to anyone, nothing
gets deleted, nothing is sent on your behalf, in any mode. The box was never the
thing protecting you.

*Separately.*

## The business interview

Its own session, whenever you've got 10 minutes. Start it,
then rename it — three dots, rename — to **My Business Interview**, so you can
find it again when you want to add more.

> "Interview me about my business."

**Recommendation: talk, don't type.** Hit the dictate button. It's two goes, about five minutes
each, and they're deliberately different. **Do the first one now and the second
whenever** — one is worth far more than none:

**First — explain the business like you're inducting a brand new employee.**

- Who you are and why you opened it
- What your product is, what you sell, and who it's for
- What the business stands for — its ethos and values
- What happens day to day, and who does what
- How many sites you run, and whether they work the same way
- What software you use, with a line on what each one is for

**Then — same business, but talk like you're explaining it to a business mentor or
a partner.**

- The state of the business today and where you want to be in 12 months time
- How you generate leads, and separately how you sell — the path from never
  having heard of you to being a regular
- What's not working, what takes too long, what you've given up on
- **Which numbers you regularly watch, and what they actually mean to you** — your own
  definition, not the software's
- Any number that has ever told you something that turned out to be wrong
- **Where you think Claude could be worth most** — getting time back for you and
  your team, and helping the business grow

Be honest rather than polished.

Ramble in both. The tangents are the point. And you don't have to do both in one
sitting — come back to the same session whenever.

It's the single thing that makes everything else better, and it's also the easiest
to put off. Do it in the car.

## Your nuance is usually what's missing

**The more you put in, the more you get out.** Early on Claude has the systems but
not the meaning — and almost every business runs on definitions that live in the
owner's head and nowhere else. Your software has its own definitions, and they are
rarely the ones you'd use.

The questions have the same shape in every business, even though the words change:

- **When does a thing count?** A sale on the day it's quoted, booked, paid or
  delivered. A job finished when the work's done or when the invoice clears. A cover
  when the table's booked, seated, or when they actually ate.
- **What state is someone in, and who decides?** A lead that's gone warm. A customer
  who's lapsed rather than left. A quote that's dead rather than pending. Almost
  none of these are a field in the software.
- **Which money is yours.** Before or after refunds, discounts, credits, tips,
  deposits and delivery commission — and whose revenue it is when someone else
  collected it.
- **Which hours count.** On site, travelling, quoting, prepping, cashing up,
  rostered-but-sent-home. The payroll answer and the costing answer are rarely the
  same.
- **What a period means.** The calendar month, your billing cycle, the roster week,
  or trade that ran past midnight.
- **Who's the same person.** One household, two phone numbers. A company and the
  three people who book under it. The same table under a different name.
- **What doesn't count at all.** Staff meals, warranty callbacks, wastage, internal
  transfers, complimentary memberships and subscriptions, your own test bookings. Every business has a few, and they quietly
  distort everything.
  line here that nothing in the software records.
- **Your funnel steps and what each one actually means.** Not the stage names in the
  CRM — what has to have happened for someone to really be at that step.
- **What counts as a sale, and when.** The day they paid, the day they signed, or
  the day they started? Those are three different numbers.
- **What counts as someone leaving.** The day they told you, or the day it takes
  effect? They're different people in different months.
- **Who counts as active.** Paying but not showing up? Paused? Still inside a
  minimum term? Every business answers this differently.
- **Which money counts.** Before or after refunds, discounts, credits and deposits —
  and whose revenue it is when someone else collected it.
- **What a month means.** The calendar, or your billing cycle, or the roster week.

**You don't have to do this up front.** It comes out naturally when you build
something — Claude will ask, because the questions are the point. **Answer them
properly the first time and you never explain it again:** it goes into your business
notes and every future session starts from it.

This is also the difference between a report that's right and one that looks right.
A number built on the software's definition instead of yours is wrong in a way
nobody catches for months.

### Before you close a session, keep what it worked out

A session where you explained something, settled a definition or solved a problem is
worth more than the answer it gave you. **Don't just close it.** One line at the end
is enough:

> "Save what we worked out here so you don't have to ask me again."

Claude writes the durable parts into your business notes and its memory — your
definitions, the traps, the things you said never to do without checking. Every
later session starts from them.

**If it's a job you'll do again, make it a skill instead.** A definition is a fact;
a monthly report, a way you word replies, a sequence that has to happen in order is
a *procedure* — and a skill means you never re-explain the steps:

> "Turn what we just did into a skill so I can ask for it by name next time."

**And if a session is getting long and you want to carry on tomorrow**, type
`/handover` before you stop. It writes down where you got to, so a fresh session
picks up with "read HANDOVER.md and carry on" instead of you explaining it again.

## Four things that are true

**Start where it hurts.** The system that costs you the most time, or annoys you
most, is your first connection. Not whatever's easiest, and not whatever's first on
someone else's list.

**Do one cheap one first anyway.** Ten minutes on your email teaches you what
connecting actually feels like — the permission boxes, the sign-in, what Claude
does when it gets stuck. Much better than learning that on the hard one.

**Two beats one, by a long way.** One connected system is a slower way of looking
at a screen you already have. The interesting questions all need two things looked
at together. Until you've got two, you haven't really started.

**The ones that charge can generally wait.** Some systems charge for the connection
— accounting is the usual one, Xero included, at around $10 a month. Most of what
you'll want answered in the first month doesn't need them. **Unless that's the core
area you want to start with** — then pay it and start there, because starting where
it hurts beats saving ten dollars.

## The systems, and what each one buys you

Pick from this. There's no right order — there's only what matters in your
business.

| System | What it unlocks | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| **Email** | Summarising enquiries, finding things fast, drafting replies | free |
| **Bookings, jobs, members** — whatever runs the operation | What actually happens day to day. Most questions start here | usually free |
| **CRM / where leads land** | Which enquiries became customers, and which didn't | usually free |
| **Till / payments** | What actually got paid, as opposed to what the booking system thinks | usually free |
| **Rostering** | What staff time costs against what you took | usually free |
| **Team chat** (Slack, Teams) | Shift notes and the daily back-and-forth. Often the richest thing you own and nobody realises | free |
| **Ads accounts** | What a customer actually cost, not what a lead cost | free |
| **Accounting** | Expenses, forgotten subscriptions, real profit | **~$10/mo per company** |

**Ads accounts are the one that catches people out.** Not the connecting — Claude
will walk you through that part. It's whose account it is. Agencies and business
partners can routinely hold the real access, invitations get sent to an address
nobody checks and quietly expire, and you can appear to have full access while the
account that actually spends the money sits somewhere you can't reach. **Check you
can see the ad account in your own name before you start**, and if you can't,
that's the first job — not the connection.

## Three things, in this order

Three things worth doing in this order, once something is talking to Claude.

### Test it against a month you already know

Before trusting any of it:

> "What were my total sales in [a month you know], and how does that compare to
> the month before, and to the same month last year?"

If the number is wrong, say so and find out why. **A report that's wrong once is
never trusted again** — much better to find it now. Often the explanation is the
interesting part: something counted differently than you assumed, or it's missing
your specific nuance.

**No figure for last year isn't a failure.** A system you've just connected, a
business newer than that, or a switch of software all mean the history simply isn't
there. Claude should say so rather than produce a number from nowhere.

### Ask what it still can't see

> "Based on what you're connected to, what questions about my business can you NOT
> answer? What's missing?"

The most underrated prompt here. It tells you what to connect next, in priority
order, based on your actual business rather than a generic list.

**Worth doing after the interview.** Until then Claude only knows what you've
connected, not what you'd actually want answered — so the list it gives you comes
back generic, which is the one thing this prompt is supposed to avoid.

### Build the first thing

This is the whole point of it all, and the easiest place to start is obvious once
someone says it:

**That report you run every month — or the one you know you should be running and
never get to.** The spreadsheet you rebuild by hand. The numbers you pull together
the night before a meeting. The thing you've been meaning to set up since January.

Start there. You already know what it should say, which means you'll spot
immediately if it's wrong.

**Talk it through — don't write one perfect instruction.** Tell it what you want
the way you'd explain it to a new manager:

> "Every month I pull together how many new customers we got, how many left, and
> what we took. It takes me about two hours and I hate it. Can you do it? Ask me
> whatever you need to understand how I count those things."

Then watch it work. It'll ask you questions — how do you count someone who paid
late, does a refund come off that month or the original one. **Those questions are
the valuable part.** They're the definitions that live in your head and nowhere
else, and once you've answered them they stop having to be re-explained.

When it's right and you trust it:

> "Make this arrive on the first of the month without me asking."

That's the week done. You've gone from doing it by hand to it arriving on its own.

**Or start from what annoys you.**

If no report comes to mind, go the other way. **What's bugged you about your CRM,
or your booking system, for months?**

Everyone has quietly accepted something broken and stopped noticing it:

- The same person in there four times under three email addresses
- No way to tell which ad or which referral a lead actually came from
- Staff never fill in the one field you need, so the reports are useless
- The report it gives you isn't the report you want, and never has been
- You export to a spreadsheet every single time because the system can't do it

Hand the problem over rather than asking for a feature:

> "Here's what drives me mad about our CRM: [the thing]. I've just put up with it
> for years. Is there anything you can do about it?"

Some of these it fixes outright. Some it works around. Some it can't touch and it
will tell you so. All three are better than putting up with it.

The ones it can't fix are worth knowing too — that's a real answer about your
software, and it'll come up again when you're deciding whether to change systems.

## What good looks like when you're through this

- Two or three systems connected, chosen by you
- One number checked against something you already knew
- **One thing built that you'll actually use**
- A list of what to connect next, in your own order

The last two matter most. Connecting systems is setup. Building the first thing is
where it starts paying for itself.

## One last thing

**What you've got now will do more than you'll ever think to ask it.** The limit
isn't the tool. It's knowing where to point it.

That part doesn't transfer.

It's the same call you already make every week, though — what matters most, on less
information than you'd like. You've always done the hard half. This does the rest.
