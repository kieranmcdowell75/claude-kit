# Choosing a model

Bottom right of the window there's a model name and a thinking level. Most people
either never touch it or fiddle with it constantly. Both are a mistake, but only
just.

## The short version

**Leave it on the best model at medium thinking.** That covers almost everything
you'll do, and the difference between "pretty good" and "the best available" shows
up exactly where it matters — judgement calls, messy data, writing something a
customer will read.

Then make two exceptions.

## Turn thinking up when being wrong is expensive

A pricing decision. A number going to your accountant. Anything where you'd want a
second opinion if a person had produced it.

Higher thinking means it works the problem harder before answering. It costs more
of your allowance and takes longer.

**Don't leave it up permanently.** Past a point it starts second-guessing itself,
and you pay twice for a worse answer.

## Drop to a faster model for grunt work

Reading through hundreds of records. Searching a lot of files. Tidying a
spreadsheet. Anything where the job is volume rather than judgement.

A faster model does this just as well for a fraction of the allowance, and you'll
feel the difference when you're three hours into a big job.

**Never drop down for anything that decides, designs, or checks something.** Speed
is the wrong trade when the answer has to be right.

## The constraint nobody mentions

Your usage is one pool shared between the chat window and Claude Code. Anything you
do in one eats into the other.

On the cheaper plans this bites quickly. The best model at high thinking, running
all afternoon, is the fastest way to find your limit — and it usually happens
mid-job, which is when it hurts.

If you're hitting limits: drop the thinking level first, drop the model second, and
only then consider paying more.

## When a job is big

Say what it is and ask:

> "This is a big job. What's the sensible way to run it — which model, what
> thinking level, and should we break it into stages?"

That one question is worth more than any rule here, because the answer depends on
the job.

## And then stop thinking about it

If you're adjusting models more than about once a week, you're optimising the wrong
thing. The gains are real but small. The gains from connecting another system, or
writing better house rules, are much larger.
