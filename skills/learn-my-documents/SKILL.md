# Learn my documents

Every business has a folder nobody opens. Policies, procedures, the staff
handbook, supplier agreements, the induction pack someone wrote in 2019. The
answers are in there and nobody can find them.

This turns that pile into something Claude can answer from — with the source
named, so the answer can be checked.

## Before touching anything

**Ask what they actually want to ask it.** Three or four real questions they'd
put to this pile. That decides what matters and what's noise, and it gives you
something to test against at the end.

**Ask which documents are current.** Almost every folder has superseded versions
sitting next to live ones. A confident answer from a 2019 policy is worse than no
answer. If they're not sure, say so in the result rather than guessing.

**Look at what's there before promising anything.** Scanned images with no text
behind them, spreadsheets, photos of a whiteboard — all common, all handled
differently. Say what you can and can't read before starting.

## Build it

Make a folder inside their business folder — `knowledge/` is fine — and work
through what they've got.

**For each document, record three things:** what it is, when it's from, and
whether it looks current. Those three are what make an answer trustworthy later.

**Keep the originals.** Never rewrite a policy into your own words and store that
instead — the paraphrase becomes the source of truth and the nuance is lost.
Summarise *alongside*, never *instead of*.

**Write an index.** One line per document: what it covers, what questions it
answers. That's what gets read first and it's most of the value.

**Flag what contradicts.** Two documents disagreeing about notice periods is
exactly the kind of thing that's been sitting there for years. Surface it rather
than silently picking one.

## Answering from it

**Always name the source.** *"Your refund policy, dated March 2024, says..."* An
answer with no source can't be checked and won't be trusted twice.

**Quote the bit that matters**, then explain it. Don't paraphrase a clause someone
might have to rely on.

**Say when it's not in there.** "I can't find anything covering that" is a real
answer, and a useful one — it usually means the policy doesn't exist, which is
worth knowing.

**Say when the document is old.** If the only answer comes from something dated
four years ago, lead with that.

## Test it before saying it's done

Use the questions from the start. Ask each one and check the answer against the
actual document yourself.

Then ask one you know *isn't* covered. If it invents an answer rather than saying
it can't find one, the index needs work and it isn't ready.

## Keeping it current

Documents get replaced and nobody remembers to update anything.

- When a new version lands, replace the old one and note the date
- Ask every few months whether anything has changed
- If an answer turns out to be from a superseded document, fix the folder
  immediately — once is a mistake, twice is a pattern and trust is gone

## What not to put in

Anything with people's personal details in it — signed contracts, medical forms,
staff files, disciplinary records. Policies and procedures, yes. Records about
individuals, no.

If the folder contains both, say so and separate them before starting.
