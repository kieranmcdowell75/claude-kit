# Draft a reply

The slow part of answering a customer is almost never the writing. It's looking up
when they joined, what they're on, what they've paid and whether they're in a
minimum term — then writing around whatever you find.

Everything is connected. Look it up first, then write a draft that's already
correct on the facts.

**Never send it.** Put it in the session as a block the owner can copy. That rule
doesn't bend, even if they ask — see their safety rules.

## Before writing a word

**Find out who this actually is.** Search the connected systems by name, email and
phone. Don't rely on one — people sign up with one address and email from another.
A name is a guess until two sources agree.

**Pull the facts that change the answer:**

- When did they start, and what are they on
- What have they paid, and is anything outstanding
- Any minimum term or notice period, and whether it has passed
- What happened recently — last visit, last booking, last contact
- Have they raised this before

**Say what you found, briefly, before the draft.** The owner needs to see the facts
to know whether the reply is right. Three or four lines, not a report.

**If something doesn't add up, say so rather than writing around it.** Two records
for the same person, a payment that doesn't match, a date that can't be right —
flag it. A confident reply built on a wrong fact is worse than no draft.

## The reply itself

**Answer the question in the first line.** Not "thanks for reaching out" — the
answer. People reading on a phone see two lines before deciding how they feel.

**Say what happens next and when.** A reply with no next step generates another
email.

**Short.** Four or five sentences for most things. Long replies read as defensive
even when they aren't.

**Their words, not corporate ones.** Write how the owner talks. If you've seen
their previous messages, match that — it's their relationship, not yours.

**Never explain the policy before answering.** Answer, then give the reason in one
line if it helps. Policy-first reads as a brush-off.

## Formatting, because it'll be read on a phone

- **One line per paragraph, blank line between.** Don't hard-wrap at 70 or 80
  characters — the mail client knows the screen width, and a hard wrap that looks
  tidy on a laptop reflows into mush on a phone.
- **No tables, no columns, no indenting.** They collapse.
- **Numbers and dates on their own line** if they matter, so they survive reflow.
- **Check it the way they'll read it.** If the owner reads mail on their phone,
  that's the test — not how it looks in the session.

If you're building a **mailto link** rather than plain text, three things bite:
don't percent-encode the `@` or Gmail opens with an empty To field; use CRLF not
bare newlines or iOS Mail collapses the whole thing into one paragraph; and don't
hard-wrap the body. All three look perfect on a laptop.

## Situations worth handling differently

**Cancellation.** Check the minimum term and notice period *before* writing. Don't
calculate a date the system already holds — look it up. Acknowledge, confirm the
actual end date, say what happens to any payment still due. Never argue.

**Complaint.** Address the specific thing, not complaints in general. Say what
you'll do, not how sorry you are. One apology, early, then move to the fix.

**Refund request.** Facts first — what they paid, when, what for. Give the answer
plainly. If it's no, say why in one line and offer whatever you can.

**Payment chase.** Friendliest version that still states the amount and the date.
Most late payments are an expired card, not a decision.

**Win-back or follow-up.** Reference something real — when they last came in, what
they were on. A generic "we miss you" reads as a mailout.

## When you've written it

Show the draft, and underneath it in one line: who it's to, what facts it's built
on, and anything you weren't sure about.

Then stop. The owner sends it.
