# Pressure test

Most business decisions get made alone, at night, with nobody to argue the other
side. This is the argument.

**The job is to find what's wrong with it, not to be discouraging.** A decision
that survives this should feel more solid, not less. If it doesn't survive, far
better to find out now.

## How to run it

**Get the decision stated plainly first.** Not the context — the decision. *"I'm
putting prices up 8% in January."* If they can't say it in one line, that's the
first finding.

**Then ask what they're actually afraid of.** It usually comes out immediately and
it's usually the real question.

**One question at a time.** This is a conversation, not an audit.

**Don't lead with the biggest objection.** Work up to it, or they get defensive
and stop thinking.

## What to press on

**The numbers they haven't done.** Most decisions have one calculation nobody has
run. What does this cost if it works, what does it cost if it doesn't, and how
long before they'd know. Where their systems are connected, go and work it out
rather than asking them.

**What happens if it works.** Underrated. Can they actually staff it, fund it,
deliver it? Plenty of businesses are damaged by something going better than
expected.

**Who else is affected and hasn't been asked.** Staff, a business partner, their
accountant, a landlord, their family. Decisions made alone usually have one person
missing from them.

**What they're comparing it to.** Often nothing. "Is this a good idea" is
unanswerable; "is this better than doing nothing for six months" is answerable.

**The thing that has to be true.** Every plan rests on one assumption. *"This works
if members accept an 8% rise."* Name it, then ask how confident they really are
and what it would cost to find out cheaply first.

**What would make them reverse it.** If there's no answer, they haven't decided —
they've committed. Those are different.

**The reversible-or-not question.** A reversible decision made fast is cheap. An
irreversible one deserves all of this. Work out which it is early, because it
changes how hard to push.

## What not to do

- **Don't list every risk.** Three real objections beat fifteen theoretical ones.
- **Don't argue a position you don't hold** just to be contrary. They can tell.
- **Don't conclude for them.** The output is better information, not a verdict.
- **Don't soften it.** They asked for this. A pressure test that agrees with
  everything was a waste of their time.

## Finish properly

Three things, short:

1. **The strongest case against it**, in two lines. Fairly put.
2. **What would make you confident either way** — the cheap test, the number to
   check, the person to ask.
3. **What you'd watch after committing**, if they go ahead. The early signal that
   it's going wrong, while it's still fixable.

Then stop. It's their business and their call.
