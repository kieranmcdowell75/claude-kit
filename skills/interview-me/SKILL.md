# Get to know the business

Everything downstream is better after this. Reports, connections, what gets built,
what Claude stops getting wrong.

**Two passes of about five minutes, and they do not have to be on the same day.**
Never ask for half an hour up front — that is how it gets put off. Take the first
pass whenever they start, and make it easy to come back for the second.

**This belongs in its own session**, named something like *My Business Interview*, so
they can leave it and come back. Never run it as part of setup — setup should
finish fast.

## Two passes, two different audiences

Don't do it as one long ramble. Run it as two, because people talk completely
differently depending on who they think they're explaining it to.

**Pass one gets you how the business works. Pass two gets you what's wrong with
it.** You need both, and asking for both at once gets you neither.

Tell them up front it's two goes, each about five minutes, that the second can wait
for another day, and that the dictate button is the way to do it — their phone and
their Mac both have one.

### Pass one — explain it like I've just joined

> "First one: tell me about the business like you're inducting a brand new
> employee — who you are and why you opened it, what you sell and who for, what the
> place stands for, how it runs day to day and what software it uses. What it actually is, what you sell, how a customer goes from
> never having heard of you to being a regular. What happens day to day, who does
> what, what software it all runs on.
>
> I'm trying to understand your actual product and the mechanics of how it works.
> Ramble — the tangents are usually the useful part."

Then let them go. Don't interrupt.

This pass should give you: what they sell, how the operation runs, the systems by
name, who the customers are and how they arrive, and the numbers they watch.

### Pass two — talk to me like a business partner

> "Now a different one. Same business, but talk to me like I'm your business
> mentor or a partner who's just come in — someone you'd be straight with.
>
> What's actually not working. The pain points. The things that take too long or
> that you've given up on. What you reckon the fix is, if you've got a view.
> Where you want this business to be. And where you think Claude could actually
> help, if anywhere.
>
> Be honest rather than polished. This is the half that decides what we build."

Let them go again, and let this one run longer if it wants to.

This pass should give you: what's broken, what's manual, what they've stopped
even trying to fix, their goals, and the first things worth building.

**This is where the gold is.** Nobody tells a new starter that a report has never
been right. They'll tell a partner.

## What you're listening for

A checklist for *you*, not a script for them. Tick things off as they come up
across both passes, then ask only about what's left.

**From pass one:**

1. **Who they are and why they opened it.** The reason the business exists, in
   their words. It shapes how everything you write for them should sound.
2. **What the business does, who it's for, and where.** The product, the target
   customer, how long, how many sites, and whether those sites work the same way.
3. **What it stands for.** Ethos and values, if they have them in words.
4. **Their part in it, and who does what.** What fills their week, and who else
   is in the business.
5. **The software**, by exact product name, with a line on what each one is for.
   **Don't turn it into a ranking:** these systems measure different things, so
   don't record one as outranking the others.
6. **How a customer gets from never having heard of them to being a regular.**

**From pass two:**

7. **Which numbers they watch, and what each one actually means to them.** Their
   definition, in their words — usually not the software's. **The single most
   valuable thing in the whole conversation**, and it only comes out here: an
   induction gets the list, nobody tells a new starter that a number doesn't mean
   what it says.

8. **A number that has misled them.** Where the traps come from. If it doesn't
   come up, ask straight out — *"has a report ever told you something that turned
   out to be wrong?"*
9. **What they do by hand and wish they didn't.** With rough times.
10. **A question they've never been able to answer.** Usually needs two systems at
   once. Write it down in their words.
11. **What's bugged them about their systems for ages.** The thing they've stopped
   noticing.
12. **Where they want the business to be**, and **where they think Claude could be
    worth most** — asked as two separate things, because owners answer them
    differently: time back for them and their team, and helping the business grow.
    Their answer here is the shortlist for what gets built first.

**Ask directly at the end, if they haven't said:**

13. **Anything you should never do without checking first**, and **who else will
    use this** and what they should see.

## While they talk

- **Take it as said, not as gospel.** If two things they say don't fit together,
  note it and ask at the end rather than correcting them mid-flow.
- **Catch the throwaway lines.** *"Not that that number's ever right"* is worth
  more than the paragraph around it.
- **Don't fill gaps with sensible guesses.** A blank is information.

## Then reflect it back

Before writing anything, give them a short plain-English version of what you
understood — what the business is, what runs it, what they said doesn't work.
Ask what's wrong.

**Expect corrections.** That's the conversation working, not failing.

## Then write it up

1. **Their business folder's `CLAUDE.md`**, using `CLAUDE-md-template.md` as the
   shape and their own words as the content. No square brackets left in, nothing
   padded out with guesses, anything they had no answer for left out.
2. **Save the durable things to memory** — their definitions, the traps, the
   never-do-withouts. These are what stop a confident wrong answer in six months.
3. **Their list** — the things from 9, 10 and 11, in the order you'd tackle them,
   one line each on what it would need connected. That's their roadmap and they
   chose it themselves.

Finish in two lines: what you now know, and where it's written down.

## Then hand over one next step

Don't stop at the roadmap. The interview is only worth doing if something gets
built off the back of it, and the moment to start is while they're still warm.

Take the top item off their list and point at it:

> *"That's the picture. The first one on your list is [their words]. Start a new
> session and say: help me build [that]. It'll need [system] connected — if it isn't
> yet, that's the session before it."*

Name the system it needs. If it isn't connected, say so plainly and send them to
the connection first rather than letting them start something that can't finish.

One job per session, same as everything else.

## If they stop halfway

Fine, and say so up front. Save what you've got into the house-rules file, tell
them it's a start, and that coming back to this session later picks up where they
left off. Half a picture beats a blank one.
